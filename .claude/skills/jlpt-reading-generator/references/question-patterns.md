# JLPT Information Retrieval Question Patterns Catalog

This document catalogs question patterns extracted from 20 QA reference samples across JLPT levels N1-N5. These samples are from the "jouhou kennsaku" (information retrieval) reading section, where test-takers must read practical documents (flyers, notices, tables) and answer questions by locating and cross-referencing specific information.

- **"information lookup"** is the most common type across all levels (10 occurrences), appearing at every level from N5 to N2.
- **"condition matching"** is the second most common (9 occurrences), heavily used at N1 and present at every level.
- **"eligibility check"** appears primarily at N1-N2 (higher levels), requiring cross-referencing multiple criteria from tables.
- **"cost calculation"** appears mainly at N3-N4, where pricing tables with conditions (discounts, day-of-week variations) need to be parsed.
- **"schedule/date"** concentrates at N4, where matching available times against schedules is tested.
- **N5 has only 1 question per file**, while N1-N4 have 2 questions each.

## Question Design Principles

### 1. Difficulty Progression from N5 to N1

**N5 (Simplest):**
- Documents use basic vocabulary with full furigana. Sentences are short and simple (desu/masu form).
- Typically a single table, flyer, or notice with minimal text.
- Questions require locating ONE piece of information or matching TWO simple conditions (e.g., "sea" + "2 days").
- Distractors differ on only one visible attribute, making elimination straightforward.

**N4:**
- Documents grow longer with more structured sections (tables with multiple columns, bullet-point rules).
- Questions may require combining information from two parts of the document (e.g., price table + notes about discounts).
- Two-document formats appear (e.g., schedule A + personal schedule B), requiring cross-referencing.
- Time/schedule matching becomes common -- test-takers must align available times with service hours.

**N3:**
- Documents become realistic practical texts (travel brochures, shop policies, event guides).
- Questions require synthesizing 2-3 conditions simultaneously (e.g., free equipment + onsen + skiing).
- Date arithmetic appears (e.g., "3 days before departure" means calculating a specific date).
- Negation questions appear ("which item CANNOT be sold?"), testing careful reading of exceptions.
- Form-filling questions test understanding of registration requirements and correct format.

**N2:**
- Documents are formal institutional texts (seminar guidelines, museum visit procedures, moving service comparisons).
- Multi-step procedural questions appear (do X, then go to Y at time Z).
- Eligibility checks require evaluating 3-4 criteria simultaneously (residency + age + employment status + exclusions).
- Comparative questions across two companies/services require understanding different plan structures.
- Distractors are designed to be partially correct -- each wrong answer satisfies most but not all conditions.

**N1 (Hardest):**
- Documents are dense formal texts with complex eligibility tables (scholarship guidelines, contest rules, job listings).
- Tables may contain 5+ columns and 4+ rows of candidate data that must be cross-checked against multiple rules.
- Questions require evaluating ALL conditions in a checklist (nationality, enrollment year, GPA, field of study, other scholarships).
- Maximum cognitive load: test-takers must track 10 different job listings with varying hours, pay rates, days, and special requirements.
- Implicit reasoning is required (e.g., a 2nd-year master's student planning to enter doctoral program IS eligible, even though the rule seems to target 1st-year students).

### 2. Common Question Structures Per Level

**N5:** "What does [person] bring/do?" / "Which [option] fits [person]'s [1-2 conditions]?"
**N4:** "How much does it cost when [specific conditions]?" / "When can [person] do [X] given [schedule]?"
**N3:** "Which [option] matches [person]'s 2-3 preferences?" / "By when must [person] do [X]?" / "What is the correct way to [apply/register]?"
**N2:** "Who is eligible given [3-4 criteria with exceptions]?" / "What should [person] do [step-by-step procedure]?" / "Can [group of N people] do [X] together?"
**N1:** "Which of [4 candidates in a table] meets ALL requirements?" / "What must [person with specific profile] do to apply?" / "Which [option] maximizes [pay/benefit] given [complex constraints]?"

### 3. What Makes a Good Distractor (Wrong Answer)

Effective distractors in JLPT information retrieval questions follow these patterns:

1. **Single-condition failure:** The distractor satisfies all conditions except one. For example, in n1_qa_1, Somsiri (option 1) meets field, GPA, and enrollment criteria but has another scholarship -- violating just one condition. This forces test-takers to check every criterion, not stop early.

2. **Plausible misreading:** The distractor reflects a common misinterpretation. In n1_qa_2 Q71, option 2 ("call the tourism office") sounds reasonable but the document explicitly states phone/counter inquiries are not accepted.

3. **Partial information traps:** The distractor uses correct information from the document but applies it to the wrong situation. In n4_qa_3, option 1 (8,000 yen) is the correct internet price for Sunday-Thursday, but the question asks about Friday.

4. **Negation/exception blindness:** Distractors assume the test-taker missed a negation or exception clause. In n2_qa_2, option 4 (city hall employee) seems to meet the "works in Kawayama" criterion, but the document explicitly excludes city officials.

5. **Temporal/sequential confusion:** Distractors swap times, dates, or steps. In n2_qa_1, options 1 and 3 have the correct information but wrong timing (11:00 instead of 13:00) or wrong prerequisite (registration instead of filling out a question form).

6. **Scope confusion:** Distractors confuse what is mandatory vs. optional. In n3_qa_1, option 1 (participation fee + postcard) includes postcards which are provided free; option 3 (lunch only) omits the mandatory participation fee.

7. **Cross-document mismatch (N4-N5):** When two sources are provided (e.g., consultation schedule + personal schedule), distractors may show a time when the service is available but the person is busy, or vice versa.

### 4. Document Design Patterns

- **Tables are central:** Nearly every document uses at least one table. Higher levels have more complex tables with merged cells, footnotes, and conditional rows.
- **Footnotes and asterisks carry critical info:** Key exceptions or conditions are often in footnotes (marked with *, (note), etc.), testing whether students read beyond the main table.
- **Multiple sections with cross-references:** N1-N2 documents have numbered sections that reference each other (e.g., "submit documents listed in Section 4 through the method described in Section 4-2").
- **Named characters create scenarios:** Questions use named characters (Lee, Chan, Kim, etc.) with specific circumstances that must be matched against the document's rules.
- **Visual hierarchy matters:** Important restrictions are sometimes buried in small print or placed after the main content, testing thoroughness of reading.
